# Making each site its own

Sites come out looking alike for three reasons: the build starts from the last site's files, every decision is
left at its default, and nobody puts the new site next to the old ones. Fix all three.

## 1. Start the design from nothing

When other sites sit next to this one, copy only plumbing from them: build config, content loader, SEO head,
test setup, deploy files. Never copy layouts, page files, section components, the header, the footer, global
styles or fonts. Those are written for this business. If a component would be the same as a sibling's with the
words changed, that is the sign to design it again.

## 2. Decide every lever on purpose

Each row is a decision. Take it from something true about this business (what it sells, who walks in, what the
place looks like, how the owner talks), and write the choice and the reason in the plan's concept section.

| Lever | Some of the range |
|---|---|
| What the first screen is | A full-bleed photo; only type; the work itself (a gallery, a before/after); a live status (open now, today's special); the map and a route; a price list; a booking form |
| Navigation | Top bar; logo centered with links either side; a side rail on wide screens; a bottom bar on phones; no nav on a one-page site |
| Page shape | One long page; a few short pages; a single screen with panels |
| Grid | Centered column; wide and asymmetric; editorial columns; full-bleed photos with text inset; a strict table-like layout |
| Shape language | Square and hairlined; soft and rounded; pills; cut corners; a shape taken from the logo or the trade |
| Type character | A heavy grotesque; a slab; a typewriter or stencil face; a calm serif; a script only if the business already uses one. One face can do everything |
| Scale | Huge headline, small everything else; even and quiet; dense like a spec sheet |
| Photo treatment | Edge to edge; framed with a border; cut out on flat color; a tight grid; one big image per screen |
| Section rhythm | How sections are separated: space only; rules; alternating grounds; a photo between each. And how their layouts differ from one to the next |
| Footer | A full contact block; one line; a big wordmark; a map |
| Detail from the trade | A ticket, a tag, a stamp, a menu board, a chalk line, a blueprint grid, a seam, a label. One, used sparingly |

A row left at "whatever is usual" is how sites end up as siblings. A garage, a tea shop and a photographer
should not share an answer on more than a few of these.

## 3. Compare before building, and again after

In the plan, add a difference table against the two most recent sibling sites (look at their home page
screenshots and plans):

| Lever | Sibling A | Sibling B | This site |
|---|---|---|---|

At least seven of the eleven levers must differ from both siblings, and the first screen, the type and the
signature piece must always differ.

When `preview.png` is ready, put it beside the siblings' home pages. Blur your eyes: if the three could be the
same template in different colors, change the plan. Do the same with the finished home page in Phase 4, and give
the sibling screenshots to the independent reviewer with one question: which two of these were made by the same
person from the same starting point?

## 4. Let the content shape the page

Real content is uneven, and that is where character comes from. A shop with one great photo builds the page
around it. A menu with 75 drinks is a different page from one with eight. An owner with a sharp one-line bio gets
that line large on the page. Do not pour every business into hero, three things, proof, about, contact. Ask what
this business has that the others do not, and give that the most room.
